1. What a cool flame is

According to the general presentation (slide 3, citing Wikipedia), a cool flame has a typical temperature of ~650 K and its reaction is not vigorous: it releases little heat, light or carbon dioxide.

The same slide recounts that in 1817 Humphry Davy introduced a hot platinum wire into a mixture of air and diethyl ether vapor and obtained pale phosphorescent light in the dark; in 1929 Harry Julius Emeléus recorded its emission spectra and coined the term "cool flame".

Before 2012–2014 the traditional view (slide 4) associated cool flames with premixed combustion leading to ignition of hot fuel-air mixtures, such as engine knock.

The oscillatory presentation (slide 2) lists as practical relevance: engine knock and multistage autoignition, the lean flammability limit, spacecraft fire safety, and low-temperature/NTC kinetics.

2. FLEX and "anomalous" combustion

The three-stage presentation (slide 2) identifies the FLEX (Flame Extinguishment Experiment) experiments on the International Space Station in 2011 (ref. Nayagam et al., 2012) as the first observation of "cool flames" in n-heptane droplet diffusion flames. The general presentation (slide 32) calls it "anomalous combustion" of a large n-heptane droplet.

The article by Farouk and Dryer (2014), cited in the presentations, describes the combustion of an isolated n-heptane droplet in microgravity as two-stage combustion with "cool flames". Slides 32–34 speak of a "resurgence" of cool flame research (2012 and 2014).

3. What sustains the cool flame

General presentation (slide 7): radiative heat loss pushes the system toward the transition to the second stage, the cool flame stage; a dynamic balance between heat generation and heat loss sustains the cool flame for a long time.

Extinction presentation (slide 16): the transition to the second stage occurred, for all three fuels, at a peak flame temperature of ~1200 K.

Kinetic sensitivity (general presentation, slide 8): increasing the rate of QOOHO₂ → QOOH + O₂ decreased the burning rate and increased the extinction diameter; with QOOHO₂ → ketohydroperoxide + OH the opposite occurs. The next slide presents predictions with a revised kinetics.

4. Three stages with helium and pressure

General presentation (slides 13–18), 4.00 mm n-heptane, 21% O₂, 3 atm: replacing part of the N₂ with helium increases the burning rate and, with more helium, a quasi-steady third stage appears. Stages: high temperature; moderate temperature (~950 K); low temperature (~770 K).

With more helium the second stage is suppressed. The droplet diameter hardly changes between stages because the flame contracts. Radiative and diffusive losses are comparable in stages 1 and 2; in the third, diffusive losses dominate. At 5 atm with more helium similar behavior is observed (three-stage presentation, slide 20).

Experiments: the general presentation shows n-dodecane at 2.75 atm (21.6% O₂, 50.2% N₂, 28.2% He) with qualitative agreement between prediction and observation. The three-stage presentation (slides 21–22) is titled "Experimental Observation of 3 Stage Behavior" and cites FLEX 1256: n-dodecane, 3.00 atm, 21% O₂, 79% N₂, Do ≈ 4.2 mm (I did not read the graphical content of those slides).

Summary of that presentation (slide 17): there are two quasi-steady cool flame modes, "warm" (NTC regime) and "cool" (low temperature); the helium concentration is critical because it promotes or suppresses the second stage. Note: that presentation acknowledges grants NNX14AG461A and NNX09AW19A, not NNX17AF97A.

5. Extinction: fuel and pressure

Extinction presentation (slide 16, summary): all three fuels show a hot-to-cool transition; hot flame extinction is very similar among them; cool flame extinction depends on molecular weight: longer n-alkanes have a more robust (hotter) cool flame and smaller extinction diameters. The cool flame extinction diameter varies linearly with the initial droplet diameter, but does not collapse onto a single function as the hot flame one does.

Pressure (slides 12, 13 and 15): ≤1 atm, two stages; at 1.25 atm, repeated hot-cool cycles (four transitions in 7 mm n-dodecane; with each one the cool flame temperature drops and the burning time rises); at 3 atm, a stable "warm" flame dominated by NTC kinetics and competition for OH. The cool flame extinction diameter decreases with pressure and does not exist at 3 atm because the droplet burns out completely.

The extinction presentation also reports peak cool flame temperatures of ~720 K (n-heptane), ~737 K (n-decane) and ~775 K (n-dodecane).

6. Small droplets and ozone

Thesis, 6.5: n-heptane and n-decane (low and high flash point) were studied by simulation, with 0.1 and 0.5 mm droplets, at 1 and 25 atm and with ambient ozone (O₃). Goal: to see whether a direct cool flame can be established where radiative extinction is unlikely.

Results noted in that section: the required ignition energy differs between low and high flash point fuels; without ozone, in the low flash point fuel it is hard to avoid the transition to a hot flame; in the high flash point fuel, controlling the ignition energy can yield a cool flame without a hot flame; with O₃ the reaction onset temperature is reduced; there is a minimum O₃ concentration for a quasi-steady cool flame and a maximum above which a hot flame always occurs.

Important: these are numerical results.

7. Oscillatory flames

Oscillatory presentation (slide 13, summary) and thesis 7.5: 0.5 mm n-heptane droplet, 1 atm, with reduced O₂ (10%) and 5% O₃. The droplet briefly attempts a stable cool flame and then enters continuous oscillatory cool flame.

The onset of oscillation is triggered by local O₂ depletion (below 13.5% according to the presentation; below 12% according to the thesis). The oscillation grows with each cycle. It is attributed to the interaction between degenerate branching and QOOH propagation/termination (NTC regime) and to the continuous leakage of fuel through the flame.

Thesis 7.5: at 21% O₂ with 5% O₃ the droplet reaches a direct cool flame. The oscillation is pronounced with little ozone and decreases as ozone increases. These are numerical results.

8. 1 g simulation: counterflow and buoyancy

General presentation (slides 25–31), based on Saha and Farouk (2026): the usual 1D module (OPPDIF/CHEMKIN) assumes a linear radial velocity and variables that depend only on the axial coordinate. The slide notes that cool flames correspond to low Reynolds number, low strain rate (~100 s⁻¹) and finite flame thickness.

With buoyancy, the flame rises. Without it, 1D and 2D place the flame near the stagnation plane. For the DME case shown, the peak temperature is somewhat lower in 1D and the temperature gradient is larger (more diffusive loss). At higher global strain rate, the flame moves away from the nozzle until buoyancy is negligible.

Summary (slide 31): the models were validated against FLEX data and then used to explore other scenarios.

Original: General presentation, slide 26 (counterflow)

9. Limits and what remains unresolved

Without ozone, in low flash point fuels it is hard to avoid the hot flame (thesis 6.5). Above a certain ozone concentration a hot flame always occurs (thesis 6.5).

Near the limit, the cool flame does not stabilize: after a brief quasi-steady period it enters continuous oscillation (oscillatory presentation, thesis 7.5). The three-stage predictions were compared with observations only qualitatively.

The thesis (8.2) says it explains the direct establishment of a cool flame with ozone in order to encourage drop-tower tests; the ozone and oscillation results of sections 6 and 7 are numerical.

I did not find, in the presentations or in chapters 6 and 7, specific results of the forced 3-D model (convection, heating) that the PSI-117 metadata describe. That is not included here.
